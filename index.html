The pattern I actually care about

In five of the six builds, the most expensive defect was never in the model. It was in the thing measuring the model, or in the thing describing it.

One shape recurs: a description that does not match what it describes.

  • Two hand-maintained permission lists that had to agree, and drifted.
  • An IAM policy describing itself as denying every mutation while naming thirteen.
  • A cost figure called “the target cost” that is the target cost at list price — and a verified: true flag certifying transcription while being read as certifying applicability.
  • A note about one flattened spreadsheet, written in language that read as a verdict on an organisation's data discipline.

The last two ran against my own interest — one overstated a figure I was about to publish, the other understated work I had actually delivered. Both were raised as defects and corrected in the open rather than edited away.

Two rules came out of it: a flag must say what it verified, and a finding must name the artifact it was found in. A true statement about a file can be a false statement about the world the file came from, and no fact-check catches that.

What I do not claim

Stated here so nobody has to discover it.

  • No USD figure for LLM cost. That price file is verified: false. Token counts are publishable; dollars are not.
  • The migration cost figure is a list-price upper bound, not a costing. Any organisation running an estate that size pays negotiated rates, lower by an amount I do not know and will not estimate.
  • No saving is claimed from the planner. It computes the cost of a target, not a delta.
  • No conclusion about that migration's source records. What I retained is a sanitised, flattened extract. The dependency mapping lived in a system of record I no longer have access to, and nothing here is a statement about it.
  • No error attributed to the delivered migration. The wave plan drawn at the time encodes blast radius, team availability and change windows — none of which exist in an inventory, and all of which the tool is blind to.
  • No comparison against human analyst hours. The reference project never recorded how long the person took. It is the comparison everyone wants and the one that does not exist.
  • None of the six AI builds was deployed to a user other than me, and no before/after of anyone's work was measured. They answer “can you evaluate an AI solution honestly?” — not “what have you shipped to production?” The migration above answers that one, and is separate.

How the work is run

Three standing rules, and they are load-bearing rather than decorative:

  • Nothing exists if it is not in the tracker.
  • Every defect is raised as a defect, even when it is fixed on the spot — including the ones that make my own earlier claims look worse.
  • Nothing exists if it is not in version control, with the hash on the issue before anything is called done.

Delivery artifacts — roadmap, RAID register, decision log with trade-offs, definition of ready and done, BPMN flows — exist for the portfolio and can be walked through in an interview. They are not published because a personal tracker full of half-context is worse evidence than a conversation.
